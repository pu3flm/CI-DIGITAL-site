Author’s Position — Tackling the Space

I began this project from a refusal: I do not accept a technically plausible conclusion as evidence that the engineering problem was understood. A clean implementation can still be a precise encoding of confusion. Requirements can become specifications, code, reports, and conclusions while the intent that should govern those representations is gradually narrowed, displaced, or merely assumed. My natural response is to “tackle the space”: before selecting a solution, identify the entities, relations, boundaries, assumptions, and evidence that constitute the problem.

In that sense, I think of engineers not as problem-solvers but as problem architects. This is why I treat explanation and teaching as engineering operations rather than presentation afterthoughts. If a conclusion cannot be traced to its purpose, explained clearly, challenged, and corrected, technical polish is not enough. This work therefore investigates whether agentic tools can help preserve that discipline across selected VLSI-workflow transitions while keeping engineering judgment and responsibility explicit.

2. Object

2.1. Summary

The project will investigate whether agentic tools can help stabilize meaning across transitions in a representative VLSI workflow. Its object is not faster artifact generation or a single automated pipeline. It is an augmented workflow in which original intent, specification, successive engineering representations, and supporting evidence remain traceably related so that semantic drift can be detected, explained, and corrected.

The work begins by asking what constitutes an engineering task: its parts, relationships, behavior, inputs, dependencies, assumptions, and observable results. Existing project material provides the concrete cases. A C++ helper relates a UART verification failure to waveform state. The concept-index work examines how engineering terms and relationships retain their sources. The Cadence study organizes simulation, synthesis, design-for-test, BIST, and boundary-scan questions. An RF direction examines digital control and beamforming requirements. A temporal-coherence study investigates what may change between observing a system and acting on that observation.

One planned object of study within this investigation is the epistemological and ontological organization of knowledge used and produced when agentic tools work alongside representative VLSI workflows. Starting from the repository’s existing conceptual consolidation, the study will identify relevant entities, concepts, relations, contexts, claims, and evidence; establish a controlled vocabulary and taxonomy; record ontological commitments, interpretation rules, conceptual provenance, and epistemic status; and assess alignment with applicable standards, normative references, and established ontology-engineering principles (References 9–16). At selected IC-flow transitions, the meaning expected from recorded intent and specification will be compared with the meaning supported by the resulting artifact and evidence; discrepancies, reviewed corrections, and unresolved limits will be recorded. The trial will evaluate whether this repeated stabilization can reduce, and in bounded cases eliminate, identifiable misunderstanding between transitions. The existing consolidation is preparatory material rather than the completed result; the planned work is to review, correct, extend, and validate it, and to determine whether a machine-readable application ontology and explicit conformance constraints are justified.

AI assistance will be applied primarily to linguistic and semantic work: comparing terminology, relationships, constraints, assumptions, claims, and rationale across representations. Code, scripts, documentation, and diagrams may be produced within the cases, but they are transition artifacts and evidence rather than the central contribution under evaluation. Each case will retain its own purpose and evidence. The final result will report where semantic stabilization succeeded, failed, or remained unresolved without presenting the independent experiments as one already implemented production system.

2.2. General Objective

Develop and empirically evaluate an AI-augmented semantic-assurance method for preserving consistency between original design intent, specification, and successive representations in a representative VLSI workflow, using documented cases to determine whether repeated comparison and reviewed correction can reduce or, within bounded transitions, eliminate identifiable misunderstandings.

2.3. Specific Objectives

The specific objectives are:

  1. O1 — Foundations and scope: establish the project’s purpose, original intent, governing specification, selected transition points, semantic-consistency criteria, and principal architectural questions;
  2. O2 — C++ and UART case: explain how an AI-assisted C++ process connects a UART mismatch to waveform observations and a useful technical account;
  3. O3 — Epistemological and ontological organization: develop and evaluate a controlled vocabulary, taxonomy, ontological commitments, provenance relations, and conformance criteria for interpreting the project’s engineering knowledge;
  4. O4 — Cadence workflow and BIST: describe an AI-assisted Cadence workflow with explicit simulation, synthesis, design-for-test, BIST, and boundary-scan responsibilities;
  5. O5 — RF control and beamforming: define the digital control, interface, synchronization, test, and observation questions for the RF and beamforming direction;
  6. O6 — Temporal behavior: explain how changing state and timing affect the relationship between observation, reasoning, action, and eventual effect; and
  7. O7 — Integration and presentation: consolidate the cases, evaluate their semantic-transition evidence, and produce a coherent repository and academic presentation with traceable claims, readable figures, and consistent references.

3. Project Relevance

3.1. Research Problem and Justification

VLSI work crosses several technical representations: requirements, architecture, HDL, scripts, simulation results, synthesis reports, test structures, hardware interfaces, and documentation. Each transition interprets engineering meaning in another form. A downstream artifact can be locally valid while omitting, altering, or rebinding part of the intent or specification that should govern it.

The project does not take faster reading, code generation, or document production as its primary result. Its research problem is whether an AI-augmented semantic-assurance loop can detect and correct identifiable interpretation loss across selected IC-flow transitions. The loop compares the meaning expected from recorded intent and specification with the meaning supported by the downstream artifact and evidence, records the discrepancy, subjects a proposed correction to review, and carries the resulting state forward. Requirements traceability and validation (Reference 2), pre- and post-requirements-specification traceability (Reference 14), intent specifications (Reference 15), and semantic interoperability (Reference 16) provide the established engineering concepts. Whether agentic assistance reduces or eliminates misunderstanding remains the empirical question.

The trial applies language models primarily to linguistic and semantic comparison: terminology, relations, claims, constraints, assumptions, and rationale. It does not presume that model output is correct. In this plan, self-correcting means that a reviewed resulting state becomes the comparison basis for the next transition; it does not mean that model output validates itself. A useful result must preserve the evidence for each comparison, the correction applied or rejected, and anything that remains unresolved.

The project is relevant to CI-DIGITAL because it connects digital-circuit subjects to concrete practices of design, verification, test, and technical communication. It is also relevant to engineering education: the selected cases expose both successful results and limitations, allowing the reader to examine how an initial formulation becomes a more precise specification. Existing work provides the basis for this study, so the planned period can concentrate on consolidation, explanation, and bounded continuation rather than claiming a new end-to-end hardware implementation.

3.2. Technological Challenges

The principal challenges are:

  • preserving original intent, specification meaning, and domain concepts as information moves among people, documents, AI systems, source code, EDA tools, and reports;
  • distinguishing observed evidence, interpretation, proposal, implementation, execution, and verification;
  • integrating heterogeneous languages and tools without treating them as one homogeneous pipeline;
  • making AI-assisted transformations reproducible and attributable;
  • relating vendor documentation to the exact tool version, interface, library, license, and target technology in use;
  • defining meaningful stimulus, observation, expected response, and fault scope for BIST and boundary-scan discussions;
  • identifying interface, clocking, synchronization, calibration, and carrier-compatibility questions in the RF and beamforming direction;
  • keeping observations temporally valid when the environment can change during reasoning or execution; and
  • producing a concise academic narrative from contributions that were created for different purposes.

4. Methodology

4.1. Architecture

The project uses a three-layer semantic-assurance architecture. The first layer establishes the governing intent, current specification, transition boundary, and available evidence. The second observes a selected transition and uses bounded agentic comparison to identify changes in meaning, omissions, contradictions, ambiguity, or unsupported assumptions. The third records the expected meaning, observed meaning, discrepancy, reviewed correction or unresolved state, and resulting semantic state. The resulting state becomes the reference for the next evaluated transition.

This architecture organizes the study; it does not assert that every case executes through a single software platform. Each case may use different tools and representations while following the same requirements for scope, evidence, and explanation.

4.1.1. Engineering Objects and Evidence

Each activity begins with a defined engineering question and the smallest relevant collection of source material. Sources may include requirements, HDL or software, Tcl scripts, logs, waveforms, EDA reports, manuals, papers, structured records, and prior experiment results. Their origin, version, location, and verification state will be preserved where they materially affect the claim.

The UART case begins from a reported mismatch and its waveform context. The concept-index case begins from source records, measured occurrences, and separately reviewed engineering meanings. The Cadence case begins from an inventoried documentation collection and selected DFT, BIST, boundary-scan, and simulation references. The RF case begins from the proposed payload, the FMCOMMS8 interface, the candidate Agilex 5 platform, and the beamforming literature. The temporal case begins from a recorded automation incident, related literature, source programs, and saved measurements.

4.1.2. AI-Assisted Operations and Tools

AI assistance will be used for bounded linguistic and semantic operations whose governing intent, inputs, and expected outputs can be stated. These include source comparison, terminology and binding analysis, traceability across representations, discrepancy formulation, correction proposals, and technical explanation. C or C++, Tcl, SQLite, HDL, and EDA interfaces may produce or inspect evidence within individual cases; those mechanisms do not define the primary AI contribution under evaluation.

An operation is not treated as verified merely because text or code was generated. When execution is required, the environment, command, inputs, outputs, and observed result must be identifiable. When execution is unavailable, the result will remain a specification, proposed continuation, or source-based analysis.

4.1.3. Documentation and Case Integration

Each case will be developed as a readable technical account containing its question, arrangement, evidence, result, limitations, and next engineering decisions. Diagrams and tables will expose relationships that are difficult to communicate linearly. References will stay with the claims they support.

Integration occurs at the level of the project argument: agentic comparison may help preserve intent and specification meaning as work moves between representations. Integration does not erase the independent origin or purpose of the cases. The final presentation will connect their transition evidence while keeping their technical interfaces and completion criteria separate.

Acceptance depends on the explanatory adequacy of each deliverable, not merely on creating a file. A deliverable is accepted when another reader can follow its engineering rationale and distinguish established results from proposed continuation.

The semantic-assurance claim is bounded to the selected transitions. Each evaluated transition must record its source and target representations, expected and observed meaning, discrepancy, reviewed correction or unresolved state, and supporting evidence. Reduction is supported when the reviewed resulting state resolves one or more recorded discrepancies without introducing an unsupported relation. Elimination applies only when no stated discrepancy remains under that transition’s acceptance criteria; it is not a claim about the complete IC flow.

5.2. Expected Gains

The expected gains are:

  • empirical evidence of whether repeated AI-assisted stabilization can reduce recorded semantic discrepancies and, within a bounded transition, resolve all discrepancies stated by its acceptance criteria;
  • improved traceability from original intent and specification to derived representations, discrepancies, reviewed corrections, and resulting states;
  • reusable tools, scripts, documentation patterns, and case structures;
  • a practical account of AI participation that includes limitations and corrections rather than only successful outputs;
  • improved understanding of simulation, synthesis, DFT, BIST, FPGA, RF, and temporal-validity relationships; and
  • a coherent academic presentation that can support later engineering implementation without claiming that implementation prematurely.

7. Action Planning

7.1. Scope Change Management

A proposed scope change must identify the new object, reason, expected output, dependencies, effort, and effect on existing deliverables. It remains a proposal until it is explicitly adopted. New FPGA implementation, fabrication, laboratory campaigns, production deployment, or a unified automation platform will not enter this work plan implicitly through a documentation or research activity.

Changes that affect the academic commitment, schedule, or final deliverables will be reviewed with the advisor. Minor editorial refinements and corrections may be incorporated during the review allocation when they do not alter the project object.

7.2. Execution Mapping and Interdependencies

A1 establishes the original intent, governing specification, selected transitions, consistency criteria, boundaries, and project map used by all later activities. A2 through A6 can then progress independently because each case has its own sources, tools, and acceptance criterion. A7 depends on D1 through D6 because the integrated presentation must evaluate the transition evidence assembled from completed, reviewable case accounts.

Within each case, source availability precedes interpretation, and implementation precedes runtime claims. The Cadence and RF specifications may continue from documentation even when matching licensed tools or hardware are unavailable, but they cannot report simulation, synthesis, BIST insertion, or board validation without identified execution evidence.

7.3. Schedule Change Control

Progress will be reviewed at the end of each monthly activity against the corresponding acceptance criterion. A delay will be recorded with its cause, remaining work, dependency impact, and proposed recovery. Contingency will first absorb corrections within the existing activity. A change that adds a new technical object or exceeds the 84-hour allocation requires a revised estimate and explicit adoption before the schedule is changed.

7.4. Risk Mapping and Action Plan

Risk Possible effect Preventive or corrective action
Scope expansion Independent studies are presented as one implemented pipeline, or the final deliverable becomes too broad. Maintain case-specific purposes and acceptance criteria; require an explicit scope-change record for new objects.
Insufficient provenance A generated claim, diagram, or code fragment cannot be traced to its source or operation. Record material sources, tool or author, version where relevant, and the distinction between observation, interpretation, and proposal.
Unavailable EDA tools, licenses, libraries, or hardware A specified Cadence, FPGA, RF, or BIST step cannot be executed. Keep source-based specification distinct from execution; identify the exact missing dependency; use no runtime claim without evidence.
Stale or temporally inconsistent evidence An observation no longer represents the state on which a later action depends. Record collection time and relevant state; refresh observations when the environment can change; qualify finite experiment results.
Terminology drift across domains The same term is used with incompatible meanings, weakening the technical account. Define terms in their active context; state necessary cross-domain mappings; review terminology during integration.
Schedule pressure Case work remains incomplete when final integration begins. Use the monthly acceptance criteria, prioritize the smallest complete case account, and apply contingency before adding scope.
Reference or publication restrictions Vendor material or project evidence cannot be redistributed in the public deliverable. Cite permitted sources and describe the relevant finding; keep restricted documents at their authorized location rather than copying them.

The action plan prioritizes a coherent, supportable deliverable over the number of tools, cases, or features mentioned. A narrower claim with identifiable evidence is preferable to a broader claim whose implementation or verification cannot be demonstrated.
