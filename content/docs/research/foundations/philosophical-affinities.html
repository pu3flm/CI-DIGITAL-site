Philosophical affinities: tackling the space

Author

Fernando Lessa Magalhães

Updated

September 14, 2026

Working map organized around the author’s questions, read alongside the Author’s Manifest, the opening project statement, General Notes, and Domain Theory: Foundations. The latter three records remain in the private source collection. The connections below are proposed reading paths. They do not establish intellectual influence or imply that the author adopts a philosopher’s whole position.

The questions can move between these areas. The map is open to the next encounter.

What is the assembly of it? What is upstream?

Thinker Opening for this inquiry
Aristotle Distinguish what something consists of, its organization, what produces change, and the purpose involved in its explanation. His account of capacity and actuality also separates what something can do from its present activity. These are philosophical distinctions, not a modern engineering state model.
Immanuel Kant Investigate the conditions that make experience and judgment possible. This opens a question about the categories already operating when we describe an object. Kant’s necessary conditions of experience differ from freely revisable engineering ontologies.
Gilbert Simondon Understand technical objects through their development and functioning. His associated milieu brings an object’s dependence on, and participation in organizing, its operating conditions into view. This offers a connection to investigating an assembly beyond its component list.

What changes, persists, and becomes possible through coupling?

Thinker Opening for this inquiry
Heraclitus An influential interpretation of the river fragment connects persistence with changing constituents. His fragmentary work admits different readings; the useful opening here is the relationship between identity and change. He predates Aristotle.
Baruch Spinoza The physical discussion following Proposition 13 examines a composite individual through relations among its constituent bodies. Changes in parts can coexist with persistence of an organized relation.
Alfred North Whitehead Attend to becoming and relations among events. A static description can prompt an inquiry into the process through which the described condition arose. His larger metaphysics carries commitments beyond this engineering analogy.
Henri Bergson Duration brings continuity and qualitative change into the inquiry. His account of experience invites examination of what an inventory of separated states leaves out. It is not a claim that engineering state models are useless.
Gilles Deleuze, including his collaboration with Félix Guattari Examine how problems acquire their terms and how concepts are created. This is a proposed connection to the author’s unfinished question about what a problem is and to experiments that change what becomes worth asking.

What becomes knowable when we try something?

Thinker Opening for this inquiry
Francis Bacon Experiment and comparison contribute to inquiry while habitual judgments, inherited doctrines, and misleading uses of words can obstruct it. His account of induction is a specific historical proposal.
Claude Bernard An experimental idea guides changes in conditions; results can redirect or contradict the idea. His discussion of counter-tests includes removing a proposed cause. Reading the user’s “Bernard… Bcon” as Claude Bernard and Francis Bacon remains tentative.
Ian Hacking Experimental arrangements can produce phenomena that become objects of investigation. This gives the coupling question a material dimension: the connection may make something happen. Naming a phenomenon and producing it are different activities.
Charles S. Peirce Clarify a concept through its conceivable consequences under possible circumstances. This connects a proposed distinction with what difference it could make in an inquiry.
John Dewey Investigate by developing a difficulty into a question, considering explanations, and varying conditions. His account provides a parallel to exploratory attempts; the author’s term “Prototime” remains their own.

How does meaning develop through an interface or encounter?

Thinker or approach Opening for this inquiry
Later Ludwig Wittgenstein Examine what an expression does in its use and how criteria operate in a practice. This meets the manifesto’s example of “interface” carrying different responsibilities in different contexts. Relevant passages include sections 43 and 198–202 of Philosophical Investigations.
Ernst Cassirer Investigate symbolic forms through which human experience and cultural understanding are organized. Symbols participate in making experience intelligible; different forms have distinct roles.
Hans-Georg Gadamer Understanding involves interpretation, language, historical circumstances, and dialogue. An encounter can alter expectations that helped make the encounter intelligible.
Phenomenology: Edmund Husserl and Maurice Merleau-Ponty Attend to lived experience, perception, directed attention, and embodiment. Some of what becomes significant in an encounter is already at work before it is put into words.
Knowing-how: Gilbert Ryle and subsequent debate Investigate intelligent practical ability and its relationship to propositional knowledge. The relation between knowing how and knowing that remains disputed.
Enactive approaches: Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, Eleanor Rosch Investigate cognition through embodied activity and organism–environment relations. This offers a connection to learning through participation; enactivism itself contains distinct approaches.

What is correctness? What could make an explanation change?

Thinker Opening for this inquiry
Karl Popper Frame conjectures so that experience can challenge them. Tests that a theory survives provide provisional corroboration. This concerns empirical theories; it is not a claim that mathematical proof or formal verification is impossible. Popper’s own account explicitly centers problems and problem-solving.
Richard Feynman Scientific integrity includes reporting circumstances and alternative explanations that could weaken one’s interpretation. His contribution here is that of a physicist reflecting on experimental practice.
Gaston Bachelard Familiar representations can become obstacles to scientific understanding. Investigation can demand conceptual reconstruction, including revision of what once seemed obvious.
Georges Canguilhem Examine norms through relations between living beings and their environments. The proposed engineering connection concerns the grounds of an expectation. His medical and biological subject matter remains distinct from a theory of testbench correctness.

How did these distinctions become available and authoritative?

Thinker Opening for this inquiry
Friedrich Nietzsche Examine perspectives and valuations involved in interpretation. Bringing perspectives into an inquiry does not make every interpretation equally well supported.
Michel Foucault Investigate historical practices through which categories, knowledge, and standards become possible and authoritative. This is a historical inquiry into knowledge, practices, and subjects; engineering dependencies are a proposed parallel, not an equivalent concept.

The cognitive and engineering bridge supplied by domain.pdf

The chapter adds approaches to knowing that are not confined to a list of philosophers. It connects cognition, requirements, and the reuse of relational knowledge.

Source discussed in the chapter Contribution to the map
Dedre Gentner — analogy A relation can be recognized across different domains. The analogy can also break at consequential differences. Printed pages 86–88.
Eleanor Rosch — categories and prototypes Categories and representative examples contribute to an account of recognition; the chapter discusses limits when extending this to abstract and functional categories. Printed 86.
Roger Schank — dynamic memory and scripts Experiences can be organized through recurring sequences of objects, events, actions, and purposes. Printed 87–88.
Michael Jackson — problem frames Examine dependencies between a designed system and the world it senses and affects. Printed 91–92.
The chapter’s Domain Theory Model requirements through objects, relations, states, transitions, tasks, and goals. Retain unmatched facts during dialogue and let a proposed model be revised or rejected. Printed 95–100 and 115–124.

These entries are grounded in the supplied chapter Domain Theory: Foundations, whose PDF pages 1–45 correspond to printed pages 85–129. Its reported validation studies and formalization have their own limits; they have not been replicated here.

Connections that developed through the agents’ exchange

  • Simondon with Dewey: changing an arrangement may also change the conditions of investigation.
  • Spinoza with Wittgenstein: continuing relations and continuing uses of words offer different entrances to calling a transformed arrangement “the same system.”
  • Bachelard with Bergson and Deleuze: conceptual reconstruction can change the question; continuity of the inquiry itself becomes worth examining.
  • Hacking with practical knowing: learning to adjust an instrument can change what can be produced and noticed.
  • Popper with the manifesto’s scoreboard: a test invites examination of the intended relation, the property checked, and the interpretation of its result.
  • Domain Theory with the author’s second attempt: an analogy can disclose a consequential difference; retaining that difference gives the next attempt something concrete to explore.

These are newly proposed connections from this conversation. They remain available for further exchange alongside The Scholastic Decree.
