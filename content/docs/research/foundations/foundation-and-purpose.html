Tackling the space

Author

Fernando Lessa Magalhães

Updated

September 14, 2026

The core

STICK TO THE PRINCIPLES, TO THE CORE OF THE FUNDAMENTALS.

DO NOT ASK TO SOLVE THE PROBLEM, BUT ITS ARCHITECTURE THAT IT RELYS ON.

TACKLING THE SPACE IS MY MOTTO (FOR REAL) FOR ANY KIND OF SPACE.

My starting point is the architecture the problem relies on. What constitutes this space? Which entities, relations, assumptions, boundaries, and transformations give the problem its shape? What makes an answer meaningful within it?

In this repository, engineering provides the concrete setting for that inquiry. The specification should make the underlying architecture understandable before committing to a solution. The scope of the motto is broader; this project provides a bounded place to demonstrate the approach.

What is behind that?

The established question is not merely how to solve the problem, but what architecture the problem relies on: how it is constituted, what it depends on, and which relationships make it work.

That question also applies to methodologies and tools. A new name or an additional software layer needs to be examined against the concepts it depends on. I want to retain the intent and meaning of those concepts as they pass into another method, vocabulary, or implementation.

The same demand applies to my own AI workflow. I should identify the established fundamentals it draws on, explain how they are connected, and state what the proposed arrangement adds. The specification should make that lineage visible.

Prototime: trying things to understand them

“Prototime” is my expression for conceptually trying things through prototypes and proofs of concept. Sometimes the immediate purpose is simply to test an idea and see what happens.

That is part of tackling the space. A small experiment lets me examine an assumption, try a relationship, or encounter a limitation that was hidden in the description. What I learn can change how I understand the problem’s architecture.

For the specification, an experiment can be presented through four plain questions: what was I trying to understand, what did I try, what did I observe, and what remains open? The experiment earns its place through what it helps us understand.

Here, “Prototime” is a personal expression from the current discussion. It is not presented as an established technical term or a new formal methodology.

Starting point

I have been developing this approach through small efforts over the past ten months. The repository gives those efforts a common place and makes their relationships inspectable. I want to explain the reasoning behind the work and demonstrate it through an engineering problem that matters to me.

My concern begins with understanding. What does a requirement mean? Which distinction does a term preserve? Why was a decision made? What would count as evidence that the intended behavior has been achieved?

These questions remain present when we write a specification, build a testbench, use an AI assistant, or interpret a tool report. Every transition asks us to carry meaning into another representation. That is where I want to examine the work.

The shared understanding develops through people asking, correcting, proposing, demonstrating, and revising. A project vocabulary becomes useful through those interactions. Its organization can evolve as the work gives terms more precise relationships and a clearer context of use.

The foremost distinction

In the manifest, I distinguish the organization of domain knowledge from the organization of executable artifacts. That distinction gives this project its starting point.

A scoreboard provides a concrete example. Its role in evaluating expected and observed behavior is a conceptual question. Its classes, methods, and communication with monitors are implementation questions. Both matter. Choosing the implementation does not settle what the comparison means or which requirement it addresses.

The same distinction applies to AI-assisted work. An assistant can help produce a document or a program. We still have to examine whether it expresses the intended problem, preserves the relevant constraints, and provides evidence for the claims made about it.

Supporting principles for this specification

  1. Clarify meaning before fixing structure. Establish enough purpose, terminology, and distinctions to understand a bounded task. Keep the wider conceptual map open to revision as people work with it.
  2. Keep the reason attached to the artifact. Requirements, decisions, tests, and reports should retain their relationship to the purpose they serve.
  3. Keep uncertainty visible. A proposal, an assumption, an observation, and a verified result carry different commitments.
  4. Make transitions inspectable. When a description becomes a task, code, a tool invocation, or a summary, examine what was preserved, changed, or left unresolved.
  5. Give tools bounded questions. Select the mechanism according to the question and interpret its result within the conditions of that question.
  6. Keep authorship and judgment explicit. AI assistance is part of the working process. The direction of the project, the interpretation of evidence, and responsibility for its claims remain mine.
  7. Challenge the starting assumptions. Repeating an explanation or checking it through another tool does not establish that the initial framing was appropriate.
  8. Give the demonstrator a deliberate boundary. A small design can serve a complete conceptual purpose. Each added feature needs a reason tied to that purpose.
  9. Preserve the lineage of concepts. Name the established idea, retain its meaning and purpose, and distinguish its reuse from a proposed extension. A new wrapper does not establish a new fundamental.
  10. Try things to learn about the space. Use conceptual experiments and bounded proofs of concept to examine assumptions. Record the observation and let it inform the next question.

These principles form a working synthesis for this draft. Their wording can evolve as the specification develops.

An engineering payload

The proposed four-antenna digital beamformer gives the methodology a concrete object to work on. The current hardware idea combines an AD-FMCOMMS8 RF module with an Agilex 5 board. The board details and integration remain open.

The project would live under a work/ directory in a tool-oriented workspace. Around it, the AI workflow would help organize the specification, prepare bounded tasks, locate relevant documentation, and connect tool outputs to the questions being investigated.

This gives the presentation a practical thread: a requirement is stated, its terms are clarified, an artifact is prepared, a check is selected, and the resulting evidence is interpreted. The contribution to examine is how that chain preserves understanding.

Built-in self-test belongs in this specification as an explicit topic. It makes us ask which part of the design can test itself, which faults that mechanism addresses, and what remains outside its observations. Those questions connect domain meaning to concrete verification decisions. The Cadence and payload working note identifies the existing BIST documentation and a proposed place for it in the mock-up.

The master’s research and the other experiments remain available as context and possible cases. Their relationship to this payload can be developed without making every idea a commitment in the first specification.

What I want to demonstrate

I want to show the verification and test engineering mentality needed to make AI-assisted workflows useful: understanding the problem, identifying what can go wrong, choosing an appropriate check, and stating what the result establishes.

The present submission can make that approach clear through a specification, a small workflow mock-up, documented sources, and a worked explanation. Hardware implementation and operational results will need their own evidence as the work progresses.

The strength of the presentation should come from the connection between purpose, decisions, artifacts, and evidence. Each included item should help the reader follow that connection.
