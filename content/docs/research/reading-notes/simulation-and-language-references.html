Updated

September 14, 2026

Source-reading record for kb/raw/, 10 September 2026. These are external references, not the author’s implementation results. All seven simulation PDFs below were read in full, 49 PDF pages in total, extending the earlier abstract-level inventory. Reading used Poppler text extraction, including visible code, tables, captions and references, with selected visual inspection identified below; it did not execute the examples or reproduce the experiments. Graph curves were not digitized. Historical performance statements retain the conditions and scope of their sources.

This is relevant to the author’s interest in architecture and behavior. The collection’s presence does not establish that any of these approaches was implemented in his projects.

Full-reading outcomes

SAGA: independent dataflows rather than one GPU thread per process

Scope: All 6 PDF pages, printed 115–120, including algorithms, experiments and references. Source identity: Vinco, Chatterjee, Bertacco and Fummi, DAC 2012.

SAGA asks how RTL simulation can use a GPU despite heterogeneous processes and frequent synchronization. It builds a process-dependency graph, assumes no circular combinational dependency, and creates independent dataflows. Shared upstream processes may be replicated so that flows can compute locally rather than exchange intermediate values. Each flow receives a serial order; different flows execute concurrently. A separate update kernel transfers next-state values and performs testbench actions.

On a GTX480 and 2.8 GHz Core i7, the paper reports modest gains for individual modules and almost 16× for its assembled embedded platform. The mechanism is more informative than the headline: added computation can save communication, while little parallelism or little computation leaves synchronization prominent. Register demand limits scalability. The claimed equivalence concerns steady-state results under the construction’s assumptions; this is not evidence for unrestricted SystemC semantics. Generated CUDA, datatype translation and scheduling are specific responsibilities of the SAGA/HIFSuite transformation, not benefits supplied merely by compiling C++.

Panda: shared language, distinct modeling responsibilities

Scope: All 6 PDF pages, including model/code examples, scheduler description and references. Source identity: Preeti Ranjan Panda, Synopsys, ISSS 2001; the discussion concerns the then-new SystemC 2.0 beta.

This explanatory paper describes a common C++ library and simulation kernel across system, behavioral and RTL models. It separates refinement of data representation from refinement of communication. A channel’s interface can remain stable while its implementation becomes more detailed. Its examples distinguish a non-suspending method process from a suspendable thread, signal changes from general events, and ordinary interface methods from scheduled SystemC method processes.

The compiler produces a simulator from models, testbench and library; hardware synthesis still requires an appropriate subset. Testbench code need not obey that subset. Reusing a testbench across abstractions is explicitly conditional on careful design. The evaluate/update scheduler can perform several delta cycles without advancing simulated time. These distinctions make the paper useful for understanding what code describes, what schedules it and what may become hardware. It supplies no comparative experiment establishing a universal benefit, and its language/version claims are historical.

Acyclic scheduling: more precise dependencies change execution order

Scope: All 6 PDF pages, including the four-process example, implementation discussion, experiments and references. Source identity: Daniel Gracia Pérez, Gilles Mouchard and Olivier Temam, DATE 2004.

The authors rebuild a restricted cycle-level engine supporting methods, ports and signals. They then reduce repeated process wakeups by adding signal dependencies that reveal order hidden inside a coarse process graph. In the request/acknowledge example, a seemingly cyclic pair of processes can be scheduled with five total wakeups instead of seven. The larger superscalar model needed 68 added dependency declarations in roughly 15,000 lines of source.

The proposed order starts statically; if active signals remain, dynamic scheduling takes over. The authors describe this fallback as accommodating incomplete or incorrect dependency hints. A further lesson comes from the host processor: checking readiness in a generic loop largely erased the gain until the generated scheduler used separate branches and direct calls. Results concern two processor simulators, SystemC 2.0.1 and a 2 GHz Pentium 4. Automatic extraction of dependencies was future work. This is a bounded engine and scheduling study, not a drop-in replacement for every SystemC construct.

Neuromorphic virtual platform: partition boundaries carry time obligations

Scope: All 6 PDF pages, including architecture, segmentation examples, component and workload tables, results and references. Source identity: Melvin Galicia, Farhad Merchant and Rainer Leupers, ISQED 2022, DOI 10.1109/ISQED54688.2022.9806235.

The platform combines RISC-V cores, memory/cache models and memristor-based computing-in-memory accelerators. Each simulation segment retains its own native SystemC kernel. A controller advances segments in parallel and uses channel latency to limit how far one segment’s local time may run ahead before it risks missing another segment’s message.

The experiments compare two uniform segments with four segments arranged around workload. Vector–matrix workloads drawn from named convolutional layers yield reported maxima of 2.3× and 3.3× respectively on the stated 12-core Ryzen host. An underused segment contributes little; increasing the quantum beyond the selected 10,000 instructions can increase waiting for synchronization in memory-heavy execution. Thus partition count alone does not explain speed. The accelerator’s internal controller handles its own micro-instructions; host simulation coordination is a different responsibility. Automatic segmentation is future work, and the study does not establish physical chip performance or timing accuracy for all workloads.

SCPEx: a method request is accepted by its receiving module

Scope: All 10 PDF pages, including primitive semantics, complete visible example listings, M-JPEG case discussion and references. Source identity: Mark Thompson and Andy D. Pimentel, University of Amsterdam. No publication venue or date is established from this supplied copy; it describes an extension over SystemC 2.0.

SCPEx imports Pearl’s message-passing approach into SystemC. Each module has one non-preemptive process and private data. A remote call queues a request; the receiving module chooses when to accept it. A synchronous caller waits for a reply, while an asynchronous caller continues. Calling a method synchronously when it never replies blocks the caller indefinitely. The model must not depend on which ready module the scheduler happens to select first.

The processor/cache/memory example distinguishes computation, a pending synchronous request and idle waiting. In the M-JPEG case, application behavior is separate from an architecture model that accounts for timing consequences. A busy bus’s message queue models contention with first-come-first-served arbitration; a different policy needs an explicit arbiter. Calls and blocking primitives also provide places to collect utilization statistics. More advanced statistics and refinement to mixed/plain SystemC were still being developed. This is a modeling demonstration, not a measured comparison of programming-error rates or simulation speed.

GPU simulation review: mappings must preserve suspension and events

Scope: All 8 PDF pages, printed 132–139, including all four reviewed approaches and 19 references; the reading agent also visually inspected Figures 6 and 7. Source identity: Mahesh Nanjundappa, Anirudh Kaushik, Hiren D. Patel and Sandeep K. Shukla, IEEE HLDVT 2012, DOI 10.1109/HLDVT.2012.6418255.

The paper reviews prior work rather than performing a new common benchmark. Its producer/compute/consumer example shows how communication dependencies constrain parallelism. SCGPSim represents suspendable processes as finite-state segments and maps similar code over different data, using barriers for dependencies. SAGA instead duplicates shared upstream computation to form independent flows. Their different reported results reflect different models, code similarity and communication costs.

A mixed RTL/transaction-level approach places selected processes on CPU or GPU using developer hints. Generated kernels represent suspension state, while wrappers exchange event type, duration and next-state information. This makes responsibility concrete: placement hints, code translation and runtime event exchange perform different jobs. A CUDA/OpenCL comparison also separates compilation, initialization, transfers and execution. Prior speedups quoted by this review are historical claims from distinct studies, not a current ranking or a guarantee for arbitrary models. The other underlying implementations were not independently executed or fully read by reading this review.

Hardware in the loop: coordinate progress by controlling device clocks

Scope: All 7 PDF pages: a publisher cover and article pages 1–6, including the example listing, ten-row results table and four references. The reading agent also visually inspected PDF pages 6–7. Source identity: Hongyun Zheng, Xianghu Wu and Yongchao Tao, Shenzhen Academy of Aerospace Technology; SAMSE 2019, IOP Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering 768 (2020) 072029, DOI 10.1088/1757-899X/768/7/072029. The visible byline takes precedence over unrelated template metadata.

The paper asks how to reuse physical hardware when simulated logical time and device time advance differently. A USB-connected logic chip generates a requested number of clock pulses, acknowledges completion and holds its clock output when no new command arrives. A SystemC thread alternates simulation progress with a pulse request and completion wait. The example uses a one-microsecond cycle and one-millisecond quantum. A separate USB path carries other communication.

Responsibility is distributed across the SystemC module, driver/protocol and clock logic. The listing’s pulse and completion functions are illustrative calls, not a complete driver. Requiring hardware whose relevant progress can be paused is an inference from this mechanism; asynchronous external behavior is not examined. The ten-test table reports “Clock deviation” values from 0 to 2 and “Scale of time, software:hil” values from 980 to 1231, without enough units, normalization or measurement detail to interpret them as precision or speedup. The paper proposes feasibility, not evidence that simulation keeps wall-clock pace or supports arbitrary hardware.

Connections across the simulation papers

The papers distinguish concurrency in a modeled system from parallel execution on the host. Panda explains the native process and channel roles; SAGA changes the schedule and communication cost; acyclic scheduling exposes dependencies inside processes; and the neuromorphic platform preserves native kernels inside time-bounded segments. These are different ways of using a model’s structure.

SCPEx adds another concrete lesson: hiding queue management does not remove communication semantics. Its queue order selects an arbitration policy, and a synchronous call still needs a reply. This connects with the author’s interest in architecture and behavior through actual execution rules rather than a shared vocabulary alone. None of the sources establishes that every design should use one language, one scheduler or one intermediate representation.

The hardware-in-the-loop scheme adds a physical boundary. Its clock controller coordinates device progress with model progress; it does not merely accelerate code. Across the collection, source code can describe behavior, express a dependency, request a transaction, schedule a simulation or drive a device interface. Identifying which role a piece of code performs is necessary before interpreting its result.

Two terminology sources read in full

IEEE1800-DEFINITIONS_ACRONYMS.md is explicitly headed “Context: UVM.” Its definitions describe agents, drivers, monitors, scoreboards, sequences, transactions, and communication roles. Its filename alone does not establish its standard edition. It is useful alongside MANIFEST because it makes the testbench responsibilities concrete.

GLOSSARY_IEEE1800-2023.pdf was read across all three pages, printed pages 1349–1351. The file identifies itself as informative Annex P of IEEE 1800-2023. It distinguishes data objects, types, elaboration, interfaces, processes, assertions, DPI, and VPI.

One useful detail is the glossary’s distinction between a blocking assignment and a blocking statement: the latter is a syntactic classification involving potential suspension, so the shared word does not make them interchangeable. Its assertion discussion also distinguishes obligations, environmental assumptions, coverage observations, and restrictions on formal computation. These concrete differences help explain why reading a term in its native engineering context matters.

The source is a three-page annex, not the complete standard. The surrounding language rules and cited clauses were not all read by reading this excerpt.

Remaining library depth

The other nine kb/raw/ PDFs were inventoried by filename and page count, not read in full: the two chronological-backtracking papers, six SystemVerilog chapters, and the historical IEEE 1149.1 JTAG standard. They offer later entrances into solver behavior, constrained random generation, assertions, coverage, checkers, I/O functions, compiler directives, and test access. No execution, benchmark, or new implementation accompanied this reading.
