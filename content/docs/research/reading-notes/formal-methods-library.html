Reading notes: AI, formal methods, and graph tools

Author

Fernando Lessa Magalhães

Updated

September 14, 2026

2026-09-10. Close reading of three external references already present in kb/. These are library sources, not publications by the author of the semester work. The notes describe the supplied versions and their authors’ claims. No artifact code, solver, build, or test was executed; no source PDF was changed.

1. A map of research activity, with a defined time window

Application of AI to formal methods — an analysis of current trends, by Sebastian Stock, Jannik Dunkelau, and Atif Mashkoor, is the supplied 95-page arXiv v1 manuscript, dated November 2024. Its title page says it was submitted to Information and Software Technology; that does not establish journal publication of this version. The core content analysis concerns 2019–2023, not the present state of the field in 2026. PDF page numbers are one greater than the printed numbers after the opening highlights/title material.

The paper studies AI applied to formal methods: selecting useful premises, guiding proof search, choosing solvers or heuristics, synthesizing specifications and invariants, and supporting model checking. It explicitly distinguishes this direction from using formal methods to verify AI systems. Its background separates theorem proving, SAT, SMT, model checking, and synthesis, rather than treating all machine-generated answers as the same kind of result (PDF pp. 3–10, printed pp. 2–9).

Its method is a quantitative systematic mapping study. The selection trail matters: 1,492 database results led to an 89-paper baseline; five snowballing passes expanded this to 540; further criteria produced a 457-paper historical corpus; the 2019–2023 restriction left 191 items; two dataset-only contributions were separated, leaving 189 primary studies for content analysis. These denominators refer to different stages, not interchangeable totals. Figure 1 makes the trail visible (PDF p. 12, printed p. 11; search/criteria discussion PDF pp. 11–18).

The query requires AI and formal-method terms in title, abstract, and keywords. Specific algorithm and tool names help cover terminology differences, while snowballing compensates for the strict query. Selection includes human judgment and criteria developed during the process. The paper excludes some nearby work, including general constraint/optimization problems outside its SAT/SMT scope and work concerned with verifying AI itself. This is a deliberately bounded research map, not proof of exhaustive coverage.

2. What the mapping actually finds

The content categories show strong concentration: 85 theorem-proving studies and 45 SAT studies together account for 68.8% of the 189-paper content corpus. Synthesis has 19, model checking 18, SMT 13, and other topics 9. Neural networks are the most frequently used AI family; some articles combine techniques, so figures that count each technique separately must not be read as unique-paper totals. The paper also distinguishes an idea, approach, realized methodology, tool, tool enhancement, framework, case study, benchmark, and dataset contribution (PDF pp. 9–10 and 18–29; printed pp. 8–9 and 17–28).

A particularly useful passage distinguishes predicting satisfiability from obtaining the certainty expected of a formal result. The authors question whether probabilistic SAT prediction meets the rigor required by formal methods (PDF p. 32, printed p. 31). This connects usefully to the corpus’s native-solver inquiry: a common answer label does not erase differences in what was represented, computed, or established. It does not demonstrate that the local experiments have achieved semantic equivalence.

The dataset discussion records 21 dataset contributions, including the two dataset-only items. Among the 189 primary studies, 124 use external datasets; the remaining 65 use random samples or do not name their source. Randomly generated data are seldom shared. The reused datasets are heterogeneous, with Mizar-related libraries especially frequent. These findings motivate reproducibility and comparison questions; they are not comparative performance measurements of the tools themselves (PDF pp. 27–30, printed pp. 26–29; Figure 14 and Table B.20).

The discussion proposes shared benchmarks, data mining, generative AI/LLMs, and further model-checking research. Its imagined sequence from natural-language intent through formalization, model generation/checking, and documentation is a research direction, not a demonstrated integrated workflow. It presents possible reasons for neural-network dominance and underrepresented areas, while explicitly leaving some explanations unverified (PDF pp. 30–35, printed pp. 29–34).

The limitations are central to using the paper well: it does not assess individual contributions’ feasibility or effectiveness; its inspection is designed for classification; the search is terminology-sensitive; the five-year window hides earlier work; and 2023 is incomplete. The paper’s increasing-interest narrative should not become a claim of steadily increasing yearly counts, technical superiority, or practical maturity (PDF pp. 35–38, printed pp. 34–37).

Two hardware-related bibliographic leads, not separately read studies, occur near the end: reference [234], “Neural fault analysis for SAT-based ATPG” (2022), on PDF p. 90 / printed p. 89; and [268], “NeuroPDR: Integrating neural networks in the PDR algorithm for hardware model checking” (2023), on PDF p. 95 / printed p. 94. Their presence does not make this mapping paper or the local semester artifacts a demonstrated VLSI implementation.

3. Seeing a large graph at several levels of detail

Topological Fisheye Views for Visualizing Large Graphs, by Emden Gansner, Yehuda Koren, and Stephen North of AT&T Labs, is eight PDF pages. The filename suggests a bibliographic key; the embedded PDF creation timestamp is a later file-production date, not a reliable publication date. The supplied pages do not carry usable printed pagination, so references here use PDF pages.

The problem is practical: a dense graph can be computationally drawable yet visually unreadable. Conventional magnification does not by itself reduce the amount of information competing for attention. The authors precompute a hierarchy of coarsened graphs and combine levels according to one or more focal areas. A focus retains fine detail while the surroundings retain a simplified global structure. A final radial distortion addresses the uneven density introduced by mixing scales (PDF pp. 1–3, 5–8; Figures 4, 5, 10, and 11).

The significant point is what a simplification must preserve. Candidate contractions consider both geometric proximity and graph relationships, while balancing cluster size and computational cost. Contracting distant nodes can introduce a misleading cycle; Figure 6 is an explicit warning that a convenient reduction may change the structure the reader thinks they are seeing (PDF pp. 3–4). The hierarchy and hybrid view are concrete graph operations, not a general theory of correspondence between domains.

The paper reports example graphs, its implementation choices, and approximate preprocessing times on a Pentium-4 PC. It does not present a controlled user study. It depends on the quality of an already supplied layout, and reports experiments only in two dimensions. Animation and integration with layout generation are future work. Recomputing each view could avoid needing a full initial layout, but would trade away response time and continuity of the viewer’s mental map (PDF pp. 5, 7–8). These tradeoffs can inform later thinking about navigating engineering evidence; no such viewer was implemented in this reading pass.

4. Native components communicating through an explicit graph model

An open graph visualization system and its applications to software engineering, by Emden R. Gansner and Stephen C. North, is a 29-page manuscript with 1999 copyright and received/revised dates. Its printed page numbers match PDF pages. The footer contains placeholder-looking journal volume/pagination, so this local copy is identified as a manuscript rather than assigned an unverified final citation.

The paper describes a family of graph tools: attributed graph files, C libraries, stream filters, layout programs, and interactive interfaces. Dot supplies a shared graph syntax; filters generally pass through attributes they do not operate on. Libgraph supports efficient native in-memory data; applications are responsible for conversions and application-specific meaning. The authors explicitly discuss the convenience/type-safety tradeoff of string attributes and the cooperation needed to avoid attribute-name conflicts (PDF/printed pp. 3–7).

Dynamic layout is separately handled through calls, callbacks, and queued events. Tcldot binds graph operations to Tcl and renders into Tk; Tcldg gives script authors control over base graphs, layouts, and display events. The same graph model also supports other implementation languages and interface environments. This is a historical example of concrete interoperability with native tools, not evidence that all solver semantics fit a universal intermediate representation (pp. 7–15).

The filters make transformations explicit. Transitive reduction removes edges while retaining reachability under the stated conditions; the example reduces a 241-node module-dependency graph from 791 to 255 edges. The authors still ask for evidence of usefulness for particular tasks. Strongly connected component analysis exposes another structure; color can encode an additional variable but may also introduce distracting complexity (pp. 15–20, Figures 10–12).

The structure-repository discussion is particularly relevant to this inbox. A document class needs an explicit model of its entities, attributes, and relations, plus an analyzer that extracts them. Shared graph operations then support queries and views. Applications include source dependencies, reverse engineering, instrumented execution traces, LOTOS specification/simulation, human/machine development processes, system-call-based process monitoring, and website changes between observations (pp. 20–25). The article describes research prototypes alongside tools used in production in its own setting; those statuses do not transfer to this workspace.

This supplies a useful historical distinction: reusable graph operations can serve different applications while each application retains responsibility for what its relations mean. It is relevant comparative material for native computation and engineering workflows. It neither originates nor proves the author’s broader hypothesis about recurrence in entities’ behavior, and it is not an adopted architecture for the semester work.
