Relational Domain Ontology

A semantic invariant across engineering and AI workflows

Author

Fernando Lessa Magalhães

Updated

September 14, 2026

1. Proposal Statement

This work proposes to investigate and demonstrate that a major source of failure in complex engineering and AI-assisted workflows is not merely procedural noncompliance, implementation error, or stochastic model behavior, but the progressive loss of semantic relations that connect project purpose to downstream artifacts and decisions.

The proposed central mechanism is a persistent Relational Domain Ontology (RDO) used as the semantic reference against which lifecycle transformations are evaluated.

The ontology is not assumed to be truth, nor is it identified with any particular solver or implementation technology. It is a controlled engineering model representing the currently accepted relationships among:

  • purpose and intent;
  • domain entities;
  • requirements;
  • assumptions;
  • constraints;
  • architectural decisions;
  • implementation elements;
  • verification evidence;
  • rationale;
  • unresolved knowledge.

The research question is therefore not:

How can Prolog, Z3, or another solver enforce project compliance?

It is:

Can explicit preservation and qualification of relational semantics reduce semantic drift across engineering and AI-mediated lifecycle transformations?

2. Problem

Engineering workflows usually decompose a project into successive representations:

\[ P \rightarrow R \rightarrow A \rightarrow D \rightarrow I \rightarrow V \]

where, for example:

  • \(P\): purpose and mission;
  • \(R\): requirements;
  • \(A\): architecture;
  • \(D\): design;
  • \(I\): implementation;
  • \(V\): verification and validation.

Each stage is a transformation:

\[ T_k:X_k\rightarrow X_{k+1} \]

Conventional project assurance predominantly asks whether the resulting artifact satisfies local criteria:

\[ C_k(X_k)=\text{pass} \]

However:

\[ \bigwedge_k C_k(X_k)=\text{pass} \]

does not imply:

\[ X_n \models P \]

A project may therefore remain procedurally compliant while progressively losing the relations that explain why its components, requirements, and decisions exist.

The proposed name for this phenomenon is:

\[ \boxed{\text{semantic traceability decay}} \]

More specifically:

loss of relational invariants across lifecycle transformations.

3. Central Hypothesis

Let:

\[ O_k \]

represent the accepted relational semantic model at lifecycle instant \(k\), and let:

\[ X_k \]

represent the current project artifact or workflow state.

The hypothesis is that project coherence can be improved by making the relation:

\[ \mathcal{R}(O_k,X_k) \]

an explicit engineering observable.

Lifecycle progression is then not judged solely by artifact-local compliance, but by whether relevant semantic relations remain valid, are explicitly revised, or become unresolved.

The intended invariant is therefore not:

\[ O_k=O_0 \]

because the ontology itself may evolve.

Instead:

\[ \boxed{ O_k \overset{\text{controlled transformation}}{\longrightarrow} O_{k+1} } \]

with explicit evidence for semantic additions, deletions, contradictions, refinements, and propagated consequences.

The objective is semantic continuity under controlled revision, not static ontology preservation.

4. Separation of Engineering Layers

A central result of the preliminary analysis is that several layers must not be collapsed.

Domain reality and purpose

The external system, stakeholder intent, mission, observations, and evidence.

Semantic model

The relational ontology constructed to represent the accepted understanding of that domain.

Property specification

The particular question being asked of the model:

  • consistency;
  • entailment;
  • admissibility;
  • reachability;
  • invariance;
  • optimization;
  • temporal behavior;
  • uncertainty.

Formal representation

The mathematical or logical encoding suitable for evaluating that property.

Reasoning mechanism

Examples include:

  • Prolog or Datalog;
  • Answer Set Programming;
  • SMT;
  • model checking;
  • constraint programming;
  • theorem proving;
  • numerical optimization.

Realization

Possible implementations include:

  • software;
  • FPGA logic;
  • FSMs;
  • comparators;
  • procedural checks;
  • human review;
  • runtime monitors.

Thus:

\[ \boxed{ \text{semantic model} \neq \text{solver} \neq \text{verification method} \neq \text{implementation} } \]

5. Role of the Relational Domain Ontology

The RDO provides persistent semantic relations such as:

derives_from(requirement_r1, intent_i1).

constrained_by(requirement_r1, constraint_c2).

allocated_to(requirement_r1, subsystem_s1).

chosen(architecture_a1).

rejected(alternative_a2).

rejected_because(alternative_a2, constraint_c2).

implements(component_c1, requirement_r1).

verified_by(requirement_r1, evidence_e1).

supports(evidence_e1, claim_c1).

The objective is not the Prolog syntax itself.

The engineering value resides in making relations such as these explicit and queryable.

A lifecycle artifact can therefore be tested for conditions such as:

requirement exists without originating intent
architecture decision has lost its rationale
implementation has no requirement relation
verification evidence supports no defined claim
changed assumption has not propagated downstream
new component violates an architectural constraint

These conditions describe semantic discontinuities rather than merely missing fields.

6. Compliance Reinterpreted as Feedback

Conventional compliance mechanisms remain useful, but their role is changed.

Instead of:

\[ \text{compliance} \Rightarrow \text{correctness} \]

the proposal treats compliance observations as signals:

\[ e_k=M(O_k,X_k) \]

where \(M\) measures a selected consistency property.

Examples:

traceability failure
unsupported claim
missing evidence
unauthorized transition
inconsistent terminology
unpropagated requirement change
architecture/requirement mismatch

These signals qualify the relationship between project state and semantic model.

Conceptually:

semantic model       → reference
project artifact     → observed state
compliance mechanism → sensor
inconsistency        → residual/error
engineering action   → feedback

Compliance is therefore telemetry about alignment, not the semantic authority that defines alignment.

7. AI Workflow Case

AI workflows provide a strong experimental case because semantic transformation occurs repeatedly:

human intent
    ↓
prompt
    ↓
agent interpretation
    ↓
generated artifact
    ↓
tool invocation
    ↓
second agent
    ↓
summary
    ↓
implementation

Each transformation may preserve syntax while losing semantic relations.

The proposed architecture places the RDO outside the stochastic workflow:

                         RDO
                          │
            ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
            ▼             ▼             ▼
          human         agent A       agent B
            │             │             │
            └──── artifacts/actions ────┘
                          │
                          ▼
                 consistency evaluation
                          │
                          ▼
                       feedback

The agent therefore does not become the authority on project meaning.

Its output is another observable artifact evaluated against the persistent semantic model.

8. Solver Portfolio

The ontology should remain solver-independent.

Different questions require different formal mechanisms.

Question Candidate formal method
What relations follow from known relations? Prolog / Datalog
What complete interpretations satisfy these rules? ASP / clingo
Is this constraint system satisfiable? SMT / Z3
Does a counterexample exist? SMT / theorem proving
Can this state be reached? Model checking
Is an invariant preserved? Model checking / formal control
Does a temporal property hold? Temporal model checking
Which admissible solution minimizes an objective? Constraint optimization
Are nonlinear real constraints feasible? nonlinear / δ-SMT
Is a mathematical statement derivable from stated axioms? theorem proving

This yields the governing relation:

\[ \boxed{ \text{property first} \rightarrow \text{formal semantics} \rightarrow \text{solver selection} } \]

rather than:

\[ \text{choose solver} \rightarrow \text{reshape problem around solver} \]

9. Important Negative Result

A formal solver does not establish that the semantic model itself corresponds to reality.

Given axioms \(A\), a solver may establish:

\[ A\vdash P \]

but this does not independently establish:

\[ A=\text{correct representation of the domain} \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{formal correctness} \neq \text{semantic correctness} } \]

A perfectly deterministic solver can produce a perfectly valid result from an incorrect model.

The RDO must consequently preserve:

  • provenance;
  • evidence;
  • assumptions;
  • unresolved terms;
  • epistemic status;
  • revision history.

This prevents the ontology from becoming an unquestioned semantic authority.

10. Proposed Demonstrator

A minimal demonstrator should intentionally avoid attempting to model an entire project.

Instead, define one bounded engineering lifecycle containing:

Purpose
   ↓
Requirement
   ↓
Architecture decision
   ↓
Implementation artifact
   ↓
Verification evidence

For example:

Intent I1
    ↓ derives
Requirement R1
    ↓ allocated to
Subsystem S1
    ↓ realized by
Component C1
    ↓ verified through
Evidence E1

Then introduce controlled perturbations.

Perturbation A — orphan requirement

Remove:

\[ derives\_from(R1,I1) \]

while maintaining every conventional document field.

Perturbation B — rationale loss

Replace an architecture without propagating the original trade-off relation.

Perturbation C — false compliance

Allow all required reviews and tests to report PASS while breaking a semantic relation.

Perturbation D — AI transformation drift

Ask an agent to regenerate an intermediate artifact and observe whether semantic relations survive.

Perturbation E — ontology defect

Introduce an incorrect or inconsistent ontology relation and demonstrate that a solver can still reason correctly relative to the defective model.

This last experiment is particularly important because it demonstrates that:

\[ \text{solver determinism} \]

cannot replace:

\[ \text{semantic validation} \]

11. Evaluation

The demonstrator should compare two workflows.

Baseline

Conventional artifacts, traceability IDs, reviews, compliance checks, and AI prompts.

RDO-backed workflow

The same workflow with explicit relational semantics and consistency evaluation.

Metrics may include:

Semantic relation retention

\[ SRR= \frac{\text{required semantic relations preserved}} {\text{required semantic relations defined}} \]

Orphan relation count

Number of entities without required upstream or downstream semantic relations.

Drift detection latency

Number of lifecycle transformations between introduction of semantic divergence and its detection.

False-compliance rate

Cases where conventional compliance reports success while semantic consistency has already failed.

Reconstruction cost

Amount of external reasoning required to recover why an artifact or decision exists.

AI transformation consistency

Frequency with which generated artifacts preserve required relational constraints across repeated executions.

12. Expected Demonstration

The intended result is not to prove that relational ontologies eliminate project failure.

The narrower proposition is:

A workflow that explicitly represents and evaluates semantic relations can detect classes of lifecycle drift that conventional artifact-local compliance mechanisms cannot necessarily detect.

A second proposition is:

Different formal reasoning engines can operate over projections of the same semantic model without any particular solver becoming the identity of the engineering architecture.

And a third:

AI stochasticity makes semantic traceability decay easier to observe, but the underlying problem is not specific to AI; it is intrinsic to multi-stage engineering transformations.

13. Expected Contribution

The proposed contribution is an engineering view in which:

\[ \boxed{ \text{project coherence} = \text{controlled preservation and revision of semantic relations} } \]

rather than merely:

\[ \text{project coherence} = \text{artifact compliance} \]

The RDO acts as a semantic backbone.

Formal methods provide specialized reasoning over selected properties.

Compliance provides feedback.

Artifacts provide observable projections.

Humans and AI agents perform transformations.

Evidence qualifies the model.

No single representation, solver, agent, or process is promoted to semantic authority.

14. Core Demonstration Claim

The final proposition to be tested is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{A project can remain locally compliant while becoming globally semantically incoherent.} } \]

The proposed Relational Domain Ontology provides a mechanism for making that incoherence observable.

The research objective is therefore not simply to construct another project-management ontology.

It is to demonstrate that the relations connecting purpose, rationale, constraints, realization, and evidence are engineering state that must remain explicitly observable across the lifecycle.
