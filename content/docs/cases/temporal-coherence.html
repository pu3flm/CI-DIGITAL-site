What Changes While the Agent Thinks

Author

Fernando Lessa Magalhães

Updated

September 14, 2026

An assistant asserted a Wi-Fi state that was no longer true, during automation work spanning a phone, a Nitro computer, and a Raspberry Pi. The question underneath it is what the proposed automation actually depended on. Following that upstream moved from Tcl and operating-system commands to hardware, execution time, and external asynchronous events, and reached the assumption beneath the operational claim: an observation collected at one moment was being treated as evidence about a continuously changing runtime.

The research manuscript preserves an edited account of that exchange, including the admissions “I conflated devices and timestamps” and “I confused timestamps with control.” Operational work stopped there, and the investigation turned to time coherence, scheduling, and computational study cases. The central question is: under what conditions can an observation still support an action that takes effect later?

Observation → reasoning → proposed action → eventual effect
             The environment keeps changing throughout.

The resulting Temporal Coherence for Agentic Control manuscript brings together the incident, related literature, saved study results, and twelve complete source files. It keeps their roles visible: an edited conversation records the dispute; publications supply definitions and model assumptions; local programs make selected questions calculable; saved measurements describe a particular execution environment.

The literature review gave the inquiry several distinct perspectives. Age of Information concerns the elapsed time represented by an update. Age of Incorrect Information also considers whether the information remains correct. Work on real-time databases distinguishes the age of individual data from the temporal relationship among several observations. Control research explicitly considers delays between sampling and actuator updates. These connections helped articulate the problem while retaining the assumptions and application boundaries of each source.

Small native programs explored those distinctions. C++ handled calculations, Prolog expressed logical cases, and C recorded scheduling observations. Tcl coordinated the study, while SQL supported queries over its saved results. When Octave failed to start in the recorded Android environment, C++ served as the numerical fallback.

One constructed case made the central issue especially clear. Several observations satisfied the study’s chosen age and timestamp-separation limits. Moving the assumed effect time slightly later caused the age condition to fail, although the separation among the original timestamps stayed unchanged. The observations retained their relationship to one another as they grew older together. A separate illustrative model compared four-second and ten-millisecond observation windows; its transition rates were study inputs, never measured estimates of the project’s network.

The phone experiment supplied a different kind of result. On a Motorola Moto G54 running Android/Termux in ordinary user space, the saved sample recorded:

Requested period Missed deadlines out of 200 iterations
10 milliseconds 0
1 millisecond 0
100 microseconds 49
10 microseconds 200

Here, a miss meant completion after the probe’s scheduled deadline. The record makes the observed timing behavior tangible, including the unsuccessful cases. It remains one finite phone sample: the manuscript contains no Nitro or Raspberry Pi timing sample, no end-to-end observation-to-effect measurement, and no hard-real-time guarantee. The study produced research artifacts; an operational controller remained undeployed.

The case bears on the foundations of engineering automation: the exchange exposed an assumption that the study could then examine explicitly. What carries into VLSI workflows is the question of how an observation informs a decision and an action taken later. The concrete work is a temporal-reasoning study on one phone; what generalizes is a way to examine what changes between an observation and its effect.

Sources: Recorded incident and research direction; study method and saved results; limitations and bounded conclusions.
